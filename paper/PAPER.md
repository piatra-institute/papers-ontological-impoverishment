## Abstract

This paper asks which ways of being embodied a person can reach, and defines ontological impoverishment as their foreclosure by arrangements for which a feasible, justifiable alternative exists. An embodiment is reachable when known routes lead to it through states that are physically possible, livable for the person with available support, and accessible on terms that leave its purposes under the person's control; remaining in the current embodiment is assessed separately. Three propositions follow. With institutions and routes fixed, income enlarges reachable sets only through price and purchased support, and across institutional regimes a poorer person can have the strictly larger set; relaxing one constraint helps only where the others already hold; and intermediate states of a transition count. The structure takes Heidegger's being-ahead-of-itself and thrownness and sets aside his non-quantitative notion of world-poverty. Experiments by Levin and colleagues on planarians, ectopic eyes, xenobots and anthrobots enlarge possibility and known routes, not access. The account separates More's original ability from Sandberg's negative right and locates xenofeminism's demands in the non-price conditions of access. Published Pew aggregates test one version of the claim that resistance to enhancement belongs to the political left. In unadjusted tables from November 2021, U.S. Democrats were more often excited than Republicans about all 6 techniques asked about, led by disease prevention, by 8 to 11 points, and more often feared under-regulation, as they did for non-bodily technologies. These descriptive attitudes do not measure commitment to morphological freedom.

## A thumb, a route and a permission

Able-bodied volunteers in a study published in 2021 were trained to use an extra robotic thumb over 5 days, in the laboratory and in unstructured daily use. Their control of the device and their hand-robot coordination improved, they reported an increased sense of embodiment over the thumb, and the representation of their biological hand changed [@kieliba2021]. The study shows that a human sensorimotor repertoire can take in a new effector under a particular training regime.

Consider what stands between a particular person and living with such a thumb for years. Four different things can. The organization must be possible, given physics and the materials involved. A route must exist from where the person is now: someone must have built, fitted and calibrated the device, and a procedure must exist for doing so. The person must be able to learn to inhabit the new arrangement and keep it working, which takes training, maintenance and repair. And the person must be permitted and able to obtain it on terms that leave its purposes in their hands: law, clinical classification, price, a provider's power to switch the device off and an employer's power to require it all bear on this last condition. Money addresses part of the last obstacle and none of the others.

This paper names the loss that occurs when such obstacles are imposed, or left in place, although a feasible arrangement that could be justified to everyone affected would remove them: ontological impoverishment. It defines the term as the foreclosure of reachable ways of being embodied by such arrangements, keeps it distinct from material poverty, and gives it a formal structure. Across institutional regimes, a person can be poor in money and rich in reachable embodiments, or wealthy and confined to a single sanctioned form.

The definition draws on four bodies of work that rarely meet. Heidegger's account of existence as being-ahead-of-itself supplies the existential shape of the problem, and his own notion of world-poverty supplies a warning about what the term cannot mean. Experiments by Michael Levin and colleagues on bioelectric patterning and synthetic multicellular constructs bear on which bodily organizations are possible and reachable for cells and tissues. The literature on morphological freedom, from its first definition in 1993, and the xenofeminist and cyborg traditions supply the political claim that inherited form has no automatic authority. Capability theory supplies the evaluative frame.

The paper also tests a political hypothesis that motivated it: that resistance to morphological freedom and to technological abundance is a feature of the political left. The test uses an argument map of published positions and descriptive U.S. survey tables, broken down by party, on excitement about specific enhancement techniques and on their regulation. Neither supports the hypothesis.

## Prior work and the addition

The phrase "morphological freedom" first appears, as far as the published record shows, in Max More's 1993 essay on technological self-transformation, which defines it in a glossary as "the ability to alter bodily form at will through technologies such as surgery, genetic engineering, nanotechnology, uploading" [@more1993]. More's definition names an ability. Anders Sandberg's 2001 talk, later published as a book chapter, recast the concept as a right: "an extension of one's right to one's body, not just self-ownership but also the right to modify oneself according to one's desires" [@sandberg2001; @sandberg2013]. Sandberg states that morphological freedom "is, like the others, a negative right" that "does not in itself imply others are morally obliged to support exercise of it" [@sandberg2001]. The difference between an ability and a negative right is the first gap this paper formalises.

Critical work on morphological freedom has examined its relation to disability and medicine. Bradshaw and ter Meulen contrast the positions of James Hughes and Sandberg on whether persons with disabilities have obligations to enhance, and they separate a society's responsibility for the presence of impairments from its responsibility not to abandon disadvantaged members [@bradshaw2010]. Dolezal criticises the consumer-biomedical idea of a body malleable at its owner's will, as expressed in cosmetic surgery and related practices [@dolezal2016]. Earle reads morphological freedom through queer and crip theory [@earle2022], and an earlier chapter by Earle questions the promise of multiplicity attached to it [@earle2021]. Hughes argued that attitudes to human enhancement form a political axis of their own, distinct from the economic left-right axis, and defended a democratic, egalitarian transhumanism [@hughes2004]. In liberal theories of justice, Buchanan, Brock, Daniels and Wikler analyse genetic intervention through the distinction between treatment and enhancement and the protection of a normal opportunity range [@buchanan2000], and Daniels grounds justice in health on the protection of normal functioning as a condition of fair opportunity [@daniels2007]. The structure below uses no species-typical baseline: it compares reachable sets with one another.

Capability theory evaluates freedom by the set of functionings a person can achieve [@sen1985], and bodily states are among those functionings: being well nourished, being mobile, being healthy. Nussbaum lists bodily health and bodily integrity among the central capabilities and treats internal capabilities as trained and developed states shaped by social conditions [@nussbaum2011]; her account of justice extends the approach to people with disabilities [@nussbaum2006]. Mitra analyses disability at the levels of capability and functioning, as the result of personal characteristics, resources and environment [@mitra2006], and Venkatapuram treats health itself as a capability that justice must secure [@venkatapuram2011]. Bodily states are therefore already objects of evaluation in this literature and are understood as shaped by social conditions [@robeyns2017]. Formal work ranks opportunity sets by the freedom of choice they offer; under three axioms proposed by Pattanaik and Xu, such rankings reduce to counting options [@pattanaik1990]. What the capability literature does not supply is an explicit account of transitions between embodiments: whether a known procedure leads from a person's present organization to another, whether every intermediate state can be lived through, and how research institutions change the set of procedures over time. The structure below adds these three elements and compares reachable sets by inclusion, without counting options.

Four additions follow against this prior work. First, a definition of ontological impoverishment as a set difference between reachable embodiments under admissible alternative and actual arrangements, with four kinds of constraint, a route layer, a path condition and three propositions that constrain measurement. Second, a separation of More's ability and Sandberg's right into different components of that structure, which shows why a negative right and falling prices do not secure the ability. Third, a layer-by-layer reading of what Levin's experiments establish, which locates them in the layers of possibility and routes, with one animal result on integration, and shows why no political conclusion follows from them alone. Fourth, a test of one version of the left-resistance hypothesis against an argument map and against published Pew Research Center aggregates. A search of the OpenAlex index for the exact phrase "ontological impoverishment" found incidental uses and no definition of the term for embodiment; the novelty claimed here is the defined structure, not the phrase.

## Reachable embodiments

### State, routes and four constraints

Let $\mathcal{E}$ be a space of embodiments. An embodiment $e \in \mathcal{E}$ describes a bodily organization together with its stable technical couplings, at the grain that matters for what a person can perceive, do and undergo: sensory channels, effectors, physiological regulation, and the devices or tissues incorporated into them. Person $i$ occupies $e_i(t)$ at time $t$. A route is a transition $x \to y$ between embodiments that some procedure known at $t$ can realise: a surgery, a fitting, a pharmacological regimen, a training programme or a developmental intervention. Let $\mathcal{T}(t)$ be the set of known routes.

Four kinds of constraint act on transitions.

- Physical possibility, $P \subseteq \mathcal{E}$: organizations compatible with physical law and with the material capacities of the cells, tissues and devices involved.
- Developmental reachability: a transition $x \to y$ is available only if $x \to y \in \mathcal{T}(t)$. Reachability depends on the state of knowledge, and it can depend on features of $x$ that are not visible in its anatomy.
- Lived integration, $L_i(S) \subseteq \mathcal{E}$: embodiments that person $i$ can learn to inhabit and sustain, given support $S$ (training, maintenance, care and repair).
- Political accessibility, $A_i(I) \subseteq \mathcal{E}$: embodiments that institutions $I$ permit and make obtainable for $i$ on terms that leave the purposes of the embodiment under $i$'s control. Its components include legal permission, clinical and administrative classification, price relative to $i$'s resources, waiting, third-party power to revoke function, and pressure to adopt.

The reachable set of person $i$ has two parts. Remaining is available when the current embodiment is itself possible, livable and accessible. A different embodiment is reachable when a chain of known routes leads to it and every state after the start is possible, livable and accessible:
$$\begin{aligned} R_i^{\mathrm{stay}} &= \{ e_i(t) \} \ \text{if}\ e_i(t) \in P \cap L_i(S) \cap A_i(I), \ \text{and}\ \varnothing \ \text{otherwise}; \\ R_i^{\mathrm{move}} &= \{\, e : \exists\, x_0 = e_i(t), x_1, \ldots, x_k = e,\ k \ge 1,\ x_{j-1} \to x_j \in \mathcal{T}(t), \\ &\qquad x_j \in P \cap L_i(S) \cap A_i(I)\ \text{for}\ 1 \le j \le k \,\}; \\ R_i(t; S, I) &= R_i^{\mathrm{stay}} \cup R_i^{\mathrm{move}}. \end{aligned}$$

The starting state need not satisfy the constraints for a move, so a person whose present embodiment is unsupported, or who is under pressure, can still reach a supported alternative. Losing support for the body one has, or being pressured to change it, removes $e_i(t)$ from $R_i$. The freedom to remain is thus part of the same structure as the freedom to change.

### Definition

Let $V_i \subseteq \mathcal{E}$ be the embodiments person $i$ has reason to value, in Sen's sense, and let $\mathbb{F}$ be the set of admissible alternative arrangements $(S', I')$. An arrangement is admissible when the society could provide it with the resources and knowledge available at $t$ at comparable total cost, and when its restrictions and burdens on others could be justified to everyone affected. The second condition is a normative filter, like $V_i$: restrictions that protect third parties, or that reflect defensible priorities among scarce resources, do not count as impoverishment, and an arrangement that requires resources the society lacks is outside $\mathbb{F}$.

**Definition.** The ontological impoverishment of person $i$ at $t$ is
$$O_i(t) = \Big( \bigcup_{(S',I') \in \mathbb{F}} R_i(t; S', I') \setminus R_i(t; S, I) \Big) \cap \big( V_i \cup \{ e_i(t) \} \big).$$

An embodiment belongs to $O_i(t)$ when the person has reason to value it, or currently has it, and some admissible arrangement of support and institutions would make it reachable while the actual arrangement does not.

Three exclusions follow from the definition and keep the concept from absorbing every unmet wish. An embodiment outside $P$ is a natural limit, burdensome perhaps but nobody's foreclosure. An embodiment inside $P$ with no chain of known routes at $t$ is a frontier limit at $t$. An embodiment the person has no reason to value does not count, which prevents the concept from ranking lives by the size of an inventory of imaginable bodies.

The definition has a dynamic counterpart. Routes are produced by inquiry, so the route set at a horizon $t + \Delta$ depends on research institutions, $\mathcal{T}(t + \Delta; I)$. Over that horizon, the embodiments that admissible research arrangements would bring into reach and actual arrangements would not form the person's impoverishment of inquiry. It is a different wrong from denying access to an existing route, because an unresearched direction is not yet an available treatment, and its assessment must weigh the value and plausibility of the direction against the burdens and costs of investigating it.

In Heidegger's vocabulary, restrictions on particular bodily possibilities are ontic: they concern entities and their properties, not the structures of existence. In the term defined here, "ontological" refers to ways of being embodied, through which a world becomes perceptible and actionable, as distinct from a stock of goods.

### Three propositions

The propositions below follow from the definition with short proofs. Each rules out a tempting indicator.

**Proposition 1 (income and reachable sets).** Suppose income enters the structure only through the price component of $A_i$ and through support in $S$ that must be purchased, and that institutions and known routes do not depend on the person's income. (a) Within one institutional regime, $R_i$ is weakly increasing in income, and income cannot bring into $R_i$ an embodiment excluded by $P$, by the absence of a chain of known routes, or by a non-price component of $A_i$ along every chain. (b) Across regimes, income and reachable-set inclusion are independent orderings: a person with lower income can have a strictly larger reachable set.

*Proof.* (a) Raising income relaxes only price and purchased support, so every chain admissible before remains admissible, and conditions that do not depend on income are unaffected. (b) Let $\mathcal{E} = \{e_0, e\}$, with $e_0$ the current embodiment of two persons, livable and accessible for both, and a known route $e_0 \to e$. Person 1 has income $y_1$ and lives under $I_1$, where the effector that defines $e$ is publicly provided with training and independent repair. Person 2 has income $y_2 > y_1$ and lives under $I_2$, where the device is sold only under a licence that lets the supplier disable it at will, which fails the non-price condition on $A_2$. Then $R_1 = \{e_0, e\}$ strictly contains $R_2 = \{e_0\}$ although $y_1 < y_2$. Exchanging the regimes gives the opposite strict inclusion, so neither ordering implies the other. $\square$

When income can buy a change of regime, for example through travel to a jurisdiction with different rules or a privately financed route, part (b) holds only for the components of access that income cannot reach.

**Proposition 2 (complementarity).** Relaxing one constraint enlarges $R_i$ only at embodiments for which the remaining constraints already hold along some chain. The effect of research funding, training support, legal permission or subsidy on $R_i$ therefore depends on the others, and an indicator confined to a single layer can register progress while $R_i$ is unchanged.

*Proof.* $R_i$ is the union of a stay component, defined by conditions on $e_i(t)$, and a move component, defined by a conjunction of conditions on every state of a chain after its start. If an embodiment fails a condition other than the one relaxed, in the stay condition or on every chain, it remains outside $R_i$. $\square$

**Proposition 3 (path dependence).** Conditions on the endpoint are not sufficient. An embodiment $e$ can satisfy $e \in P \cap L_i \cap A_i$, with a chain of known routes leading to it, and still lie outside $R_i$ when every such chain passes through an intermediate state $x_j$, with $1 \le j < k$, outside $L_i \cap A_i$.

*Proof.* Let the only chain be $x_0 \to x_1 \to e$, with $x_1$ a recovery or training period that the person cannot sustain without income or care that the arrangement does not provide. Then $x_1 \notin L_i \cap A_i$ and $e \notin R_i$, although $e$ satisfies every endpoint condition. $\square$

Proposition 3 makes support during transitions, and the possibility of returning, part of what freedom of embodiment is. A natural indicator follows: whether $e_i(t)$ belongs to the move-reachable set computed from $e$, that is, whether the change can be undone.

### Indicators

Each layer has measurable indicators. Table 1 lists examples; none of them requires measuring consciousness, identity or the value of a life.

| Layer | Question | Example indicators |
|:-----------|:-------------------|:-------------------------------------|
| Physical possibility | Is the organization compatible with physics and the materials involved? | demonstration in any organism or device; theoretical bounds on materials, energy and signalling |
| Developmental reachability | Is there a known route from this person's current state? | stage of the best route (none, preclinical, trial, approved); time from demonstration in a model organism to a human route |
| Lived integration | Can the person learn to inhabit and sustain it? | training time to a criterion; embodiment questionnaires; continued use and abandonment at one year; repair downtime |
| Political accessibility | Is it permitted and obtainable on terms the person controls? | legal status; gatekeeping requirements; out-of-pocket cost as a share of income; waiting time; third-party power to revoke function; right to independent repair; penalties for refusing |

: Layers of the reachable set and example indicators.

Lived integration already has an empirical literature. A review of upper-limb prosthesis use over 25 years reported mean rejection rates in adult populations of 26% for body-powered devices and 23% for electric devices [@biddiss2007]. A device delivered is therefore not an embodiment reached. Counting fitted devices measures one route; it does not measure the reachable set.

The propositions yield a measurement design. Take one embodiment technology whose physical possibility and route are fixed, and compare jurisdictions that differ in coverage, gatekeeping and service rules. Proposition 1 predicts that sustained use responds weakly to income where non-price constraints bind; Proposition 2 predicts that training support and coverage interact, so that either alone moves sustained use less than both together; Proposition 3 predicts that support during the transition period predicts completion independently of support after it. These predictions are stated for testing; no such study is reported here.

## What Heidegger supplies and what he refuses

*Being and Time* analyses the entity for which its own being is an issue and locates its essence in its existence; Heidegger separates this existential analytic from anthropology, psychology and biology [@heidegger1962]. Understanding has the structure of projection upon possibilities, and Heidegger treats possibility as the most basic positive character of Dasein, distinct from empty logical possibility and from the contingency of things present-at-hand. Care unites being-ahead-of-itself, being-already-in-a-world and being-alongside entities encountered within it. Thrownness names the fact that Dasein always finds itself delivered over to a situation it did not choose.

These structures give the reachable set its existential shape. The current embodiment $e_i(t)$ is the thrown starting point. Being-ahead-of-itself is the orientation toward successors. Possibility is the primary character of the person, not an attribute added to a finished one. The appropriation adds one step that Heidegger did not take: it counts the body among the things toward which existence can be ahead of itself. "The emancipation of Dasein as freedom of embodiment" means here that the range of bodily ways in which a person can project themselves is part of what political arrangements can widen or narrow.

Heidegger's text resists this step in three places. First, *Being and Time* mentions Dasein's bodily nature in its discussion of spatiality and sets that problematic aside [@heidegger1962], an omission that Aho examines at book length [@aho2009]. Later lecture courses take up the living body. Engelland reports a passage from the 1930s in which Heidegger writes that "the living body [Leib] belongs to the Dasein of man" while holding that the human living body, even when abstracted from human freedom, is not identical with animal bodies [@engelland2015, pp. 188-189]. Heidegger's body is a mode of existing, not an organization that technique could rework.

Second, Heidegger's own concept of poverty in world runs against the present one. In the 1929 to 1930 lectures he advanced the thesis that, in comparison with Dasein, animals are poor in world [@heidegger1995; cited in @engelland2015, p. 184]. He insisted that the difference "is not simply a question of a qualitative otherness" and not a question of "quantitative distinctions in range, depth, and breadth", but of whether the animal can apprehend something as something at all [@heidegger1995; quoted in @engelland2015, p. 184]. Ontological impoverishment, as defined above, is exactly the graded, contingent narrowing of range that Heidegger excluded from his notion. The present term borrows the word poverty for a different object and is not an application of his thesis.

Third, the essay on technology describes modern technology as a mode of revealing, enframing, in which beings show up as standing-reserve, available on call for further ordering, and it treats the human being as itself at risk of being taken in this way [@heidegger1977]. A Heideggerian objection to morphological freedom follows: transformation of the body would be one more optimization of availability. The objection identifies a real danger, and the formal structure gives it a precise location. An exoskeleton issued and configured by an employer to raise lifting output may enlarge a worker's physical repertoire while keeping its purposes in someone else's hands. Under the definition, such an embodiment is not accessible to the worker in the relevant sense, because the condition on $A_i$ requires that its purposes remain under the person's control. The danger becomes an indicator of who sets purposes and who can revoke function. It does not become a reason to refuse transformation as such.

Merleau-Ponty supplies what Heidegger withheld: a phenomenology of the body as the bearer of an "I can" that incorporates instruments through habit, as in his discussion of the blind person's cane, which ceases to be an object and becomes an extension of the perceiving body [@merleauponty2012]. Incorporation changes the field of what can be perceived and done. The robotic-thumb study gives this an experimental counterpart, with measured changes in control, reported embodiment and hand representation after training [@kieliba2021]. Lived integration is therefore a layer with its own measurements.

## What the biology establishes, layer by layer

Levin and colleagues study how cells and tissues establish, maintain and change anatomical organization, with attention to bioelectric signals: membrane voltage and communication through gap junctions. Table 2 sets out six primary studies by the layer of the reachable set that each informs.

| Study | System and intervention | Reported result | Layer informed |
|:--------------|:-----------------------|:---------------------------|:-----------|
| Durant et al. 2017 | Planarian fragments; brief disruption of gap-junction communication | Stochastic, persistent double-headed regeneration; normal-looking "cryptic" worms regenerate double-headed when recut; voltage manipulation resets some | possibility; reachability; hidden state |
| Pai et al. 2012 | *Xenopus* embryos; manipulation of membrane voltage | Ectopic eyes induced, including far outside the anterior neural field | possibility; reachability |
| Blackiston and Levin 2013 | *Xenopus* tadpoles; eye primordia grafted to the tail | Tail eyes conferred vision in light-mediated learning assays | integration, in an animal |
| Kriegman et al. 2020 | Designs evolved in simulation, built from frog progenitor cells | Living constructs with designed shapes that move | possibility |
| Gumuskaya et al. 2024 | Adult human airway epithelial cells in culture | Motile multicellular constructs self-assemble without genomic editing or manual sculpting | possibility |
| Gumuskaya et al. 2025 | Same constructs; growth, ageing and damage | Partial recovery of shape after injury, with limits | possibility; limits |

: Six experimental studies from Levin and colleagues and the layer each informs.

The planarian study is the most informative for the formal structure. A brief perturbation of bioelectric communication in regenerating fragments produced a constant ratio of double-headed to normal regenerates, and the authors report that "regenerating planarians' normal anterior-posterior pattern can be permanently rewritten by a brief perturbation of endogenous bioelectrical networks" [@durant2017, Abstract]. Some animals that looked normal regenerated with two heads when cut again in water, and double-headed animals kept regenerating two heads in plain water long after the drug was gone. Treating double-headed animals with a hyperpolarizing drug reset 34% of them (N = 102) to single-headed regeneration that persisted across further rounds of cutting [@durant2017, Results, Fig. 4F]. For the formalism, the result means that the state $e$ must include regulatory state as well as visible anatomy: two bodies that look the same can have different reachable successors.

In *Xenopus* embryos, manipulating the membrane voltage of cells outside the eye field induced well-formed ectopic eyes, and the authors report that such eyes "can be induced far outside the anterior neural field" through a calcium-channel-dependent pathway [@pai2012, Abstract]. That study concerned patterning. A later study from the same laboratory grafted eye primordia to the tails of tadpoles: the ectopic eyes sent projections to the stomach and trunk, and in light-mediated learning assays they conferred vision on the host [@blackiston2013, Abstract]. A vertebrate nervous system can thus incorporate a sense organ in an unfamiliar place into adaptive behaviour. The eyes were grafted, not induced bioelectrically, and the subjects were tadpoles.

The xenobot study used an evolutionary algorithm to design candidate organisms in simulation; selected designs were built from *Xenopus* epidermal and cardiac progenitor cells, and the aggregated tissue was "manually shaped by subtraction" with microsurgery forceps and a cautery electrode [@kriegman2020, Results]. Frog cells can thus sustain organized, motile forms outside the frog body plan, but the final shapes were imposed by the researchers.

The anthrobot study grew motile spheroids from adult human airway epithelial cells, and the authors describe the platform as revealing "morphological and behavioral plasticity of human cells, without genomic editing" [@gumuskaya2024, Abstract]. In an in-vitro assay, aggregated constructs placed across a scratch in a layer of human neural cells promoted closure of the gap [@gumuskaya2024, Results]. A follow-up characterized the same constructs' life cycle and response to injury: of 23 constructs imaged after needle damage, 16 recovered their original spheroid shape, recovery occurred within the first 20 minutes with no further healing over 48 to 72 hours, and halves separated by the needle remained distinct bodies [@gumuskaya2025, Results]. The follow-up comes from the same group, so it characterizes the system further without replicating it independently.

Two theoretical frameworks accompany this work. The Technological Approach to Mind Everywhere, which Levin introduces as a Perspective, proposes to understand and manipulate cognition in unconventional substrates and to treat morphogenesis as an example of basal cognition [@levin2022, Abstract]. Bongard and Levin call "polycomputing" the ability of the same substrate to compute different things simultaneously and make the results available to different observers [@bongard2023, Abstract]. These are research frameworks. They suggest where routes might be sought, for example by changing setpoints that tissues already regulate toward, instead of specifying every molecular event. They are not evidence that any particular route exists.

Read by layer, the experiments enlarge the known physical possibilities and known routes for cells and tissues of the organisms studied, and the grafted-eye study shows behavioural integration of a relocated sense organ in an animal. None of them concerns lived integration or political accessibility for a person, and the people from whom the anthrobot cells came were not thereby transformed. No political conclusion follows from the biology alone. What follows is narrower and still consequential. The planarian result shows that a body's present appearance underdetermines its reachable forms, which removes one ground for treating current anatomy as a boundary. And because known routes are produced by inquiry, the biology bears on impoverishment of inquiry: a decision not to investigate bioelectric control of form is a decision about future reachable sets, to be weighed against its risks and costs.

## Morphological freedom, xenofeminism and the cyborg

More's 1993 essay paired its definition of morphological freedom with a definition of its opponent. "Biological fundamentalism" appears in the same glossary as "a new conservatism that resists asexual reproduction, genetic engineering, altering human anatomy, overcoming death" [@more1993]. More located resistance to bodily transformation on the conservative side. Sandberg's talk took up the egalitarian objection directly. Against the worry that morphological freedom would widen class differences, he argued that the costs of technology fall relative to wages and that "the best way of making actual morphological freedom an option is not to restrict it, but rather to encourage the use and development of it among a wide variety of people" [@sandberg2001].

In the terms of the reachable set, More's ability is membership of embodiments in $R_i$. Sandberg's negative right protects against one component of $A_i$, interference by others, and his reply on inequality addresses one further component, price. Proposition 1 shows why these do not secure the ability: physical possibility, known routes, lived integration and the non-price components of access are untouched by either. Bostrom and Ord's reversal test addresses a separate weakness in the opposing case: a judgment that a change in some human parameter would be bad should survive the question whether the opposite change would also be bad, failing which status quo bias is the likely explanation [@bostrom2006]. Bostrom's defence of posthuman dignity argues that dignity can belong to modified beings as well as unmodified ones [@bostrom2005].

The xenofeminist manifesto of Laboria Cuboniks makes the political claim without the libertarian frame. "Nothing should be accepted as fixed, permanent, or 'given'–neither material conditions nor social forms," it declares, and it opposes the glorification of nature on behalf of "the queer and trans among us, the differently-abled," and those who have suffered discrimination due to pregnancy [@laboriacuboniks2015, sec. 0x01]. It describes technoscience as a domain "where nothing is so sacred that it cannot be reengineered and transformed so as to widen our aperture of freedom, extending to gender and the human" [@laboriacuboniks2015, sec. 0x11]. The manifesto pairs "normative anti-naturalism" with "ontological naturalism" and calls the work of untangling what ought to be from what is an infinite task [@laboriacuboniks2015, secs. 0x11 and 0x12]. That pairing corresponds to the separation in the formal structure between $P$, a matter of fact to be investigated, and $A_i$, a matter of norms to be justified.

On hormones, the manifesto asks who or what their distribution prioritizes or pathologizes, credits the internet with wresting control of the hormonal economy away from gatekeeping institutions, and adds that "to trade in the rule of bureaucrats for the market is, however, not a victory in itself" [@laboriacuboniks2015, sec. 0x16]. This is a claim about the non-price components of access: replacing one gatekeeper by another leaves $A_i$ conditional on someone else's terms. The manifesto's proposal of free and open-source medicine concerns both routes and access. Its closing section asks for "new affordances of perception and action unblinkered by naturalised identities" [@laboriacuboniks2015, sec. 0x1A], which states as a political aim the enlargement of perceptual and practical embodiment that the formal structure measures. Hester's book develops xenofeminism at length, with particular attention to reproductive technologies [@hester2018]. Haraway's cyborg figure had earlier refused the idea of an organic whole that precedes technical mediation while tracing the cyborg's origins in militarism and patriarchal capitalism [@haraway1991].

Disability scholarship supplies the strongest internal check on any politics of transformation. Garland-Thomson's concept of the misfit locates disability in the encounter between a body and an environment that fails to sustain it [@garlandthomson2011], and her case for conserving disability opposes the elimination of disabled lives through medical and genetic technologies [@garlandthomson2012]. Kafer criticizes imagined futures that presume disability's elimination [@kafer2013]. The reachable set accommodates these arguments and is constrained by them. A misfit can be removed by changing the world, which enlarges $L_i$ and $A_i$ for the present embodiment; the structure does not privilege changing the body. The current embodiment belongs to the evaluated set by definition, so pressure to modify counts as impoverishment. And reachable sets are compared by inclusion, never by distance from a statistical norm. Sandberg himself observed that the disability movement had been a strong supporter of the right to determine one's body [@sandberg2001].

: Positions on bodily transformation and technological abundance by political location.

The favourable column contains libertarian, liberal, democratic-left, feminist-left and socialist positions. Srnicek and Williams argue for full automation and a politics oriented to building a post-work future [@srnicek2015]; Bastani argues that automation, renewable energy and biotechnology could ground a post-scarcity economy under collective ownership [@bastani2019]. Hughes built his democratic transhumanism on the claim that enhancement politics cuts across the economic axis [@hughes2004]. The critical column contains conservative bioconservatives, for whom human nature and its given limits carry normative weight [@kass2003; @fukuyama2002], and left-liberal critics with different targets. Habermas objects to genetic design of future persons because it alters the conditions of their self-understanding as authors of their own lives [@habermas2003]. Sandel defends an ethic of giftedness against a drive to mastery [@sandel2007]. Degrowth scholarship argues for a reorganization of the economy toward reduced resource and energy use [@kallis2018; @hickel2022]; its quarrel is with aggregate throughput, and it does not address morphological freedom as such. The disability-justice critique opposes elimination and imposed normalization, which is a claim about $A_i$ and about including $e_i(t)$, not opposition to self-chosen change.

The record supports a narrower statement than the hypothesis. Opposition to bodily transformation appears on the right and on the left, for different reasons, and enthusiasm appears on both as well. The left-of-centre critics mostly target coercion, design of non-consenting persons, or growth in material throughput. None of the works examined argues for poverty as a good.

### Published survey evidence

Pew Research Center asked a representative panel of 4,726 U.S. adults in March 2016 about gene editing to reduce a baby's disease risk, brain chip implants to improve cognition and synthetic blood to improve physical abilities [@pew2016, pp. 3 and 104]. In November 2021 it asked 10,260 U.S. adults how excited or concerned they would be about six possible techniques that change human abilities, and asked half of them further questions about brain chips, gene editing for babies and robotic exoskeletons for manual labor [@pew2022, pp. 2 and 119]. I transcribed the published aggregate tables, with page locators, and computed differences between groups. No respondent-level data were used: Pew distributes them only to registered account holders. The excitement values were read from a chart image; the regulation values and margins were checked automatically against the text of the stated pages. Margins for differences use the 95% margins that Pew reports for each group, which include design effects, combined as for disjoint samples; items asked of half the sample use Pew's half-sample margins. Because published percentages are rounded to integers, each difference can be overstated by up to one point; every gap reported as exceeding its margin also does so after subtracting that point.

The 2016 report publishes no breakdown by party, so it cannot test the hypothesis.

The 2021 survey's appendix reports, by party, the share who would be very or somewhat excited about six techniques: preventing serious disease, processing information far more quickly, hearing and seeing far beyond typical people, greatly increased lifting strength, and slowing ageing so that people live decades longer [@pew2022, p. 128]. On all 6 items, Democrats and Democratic-leaning independents were more often very or somewhat excited than Republicans and Republican leaners, by 8 to 11 percentage points; each gap exceeds its approximate margin of 3.2 points. For preventing serious disease, the shares were 72% and 61%. The two party groups together make up 93% of adults. The same tables break opinion down by religious commitment, which is correlated with party; published tables cannot separate the two, and this paper makes no comparison between them. Figure 1 shows the party differences.

The same report shows where Democrats and Republicans differ in the direction the hypothesis expects. Asked whether, if each technology became widespread, their greater concern would be that government goes too far or not far enough in regulating it, Democrats more often chose "not far enough" for brain chips, gene editing and exoskeletons alike, by 26 to 34 points across the 3 technologies [@pew2022, pp. 92, 103 and 113]. For brain chips, 64% of Democrats and 36% of Republicans chose "not far enough". The same question about the survey's non-bodily technologies, police facial recognition, social media algorithms and driverless cars, produced party gaps of 21 to 30 points [@pew2022, pp. 40, 54 and 67]. These items went to half samples, for which a party difference has an approximate margin of 4.6 points. Pew describes the differences as consistent with the parties' broader views on government regulation [@pew2022, p. 103].


### What the evidence supports

The survey items measure excitement about specific techniques, led by disease prevention, and preferences about regulation, among U.S. adults grouped by party identification. They do not measure commitment to morphological freedom, desire for the techniques for oneself, or the ideological left of Table 3; the 2021 report publishes no party breakdown of whether respondents would want the techniques. In these bivariate tables, Democrats and leaners were the more excited group on every item reported by party, which contradicts the hypothesis at that scale. The party difference in the expected direction concerns regulation, and it is not specific to bodily technologies. The hypothesis conflates two dimensions, enthusiasm for transformation and preferences about its governance.

The formal structure explains why the conflation matters. Regulation acts on $A_i$, and it can act in either direction. Safety testing may delay a route; a ban on employers requiring implanted devices, of the kind Pew notes some states had passed [@pew2022, p. 92], protects the freedom to remain and removes one form of pressure to adopt. Whether a regulatory preference widens or narrows reachable sets depends on which components of access it changes. On the evidence and arguments examined here, ontological impoverishment has no single political home. Its sources include religious and conservative appeals to a given human nature, egalitarian suspicion of design and markets, gatekeeping by clinics and agencies, and control of embodied function by firms and employers.

## Limits and open questions

The propositions are consequences of a definition, and their content is the structure they impose on measurement, not an empirical discovery. Two inputs to the definition are normative and contestable: the set $V_i$ of embodiments a person has reason to value, and the set $\mathbb{F}$ of feasible alternative arrangements. Disagreement about them is disagreement about justice, and the formalism makes it explicit without settling it. The definition also leaves open who decides for persons who cannot express a decision through ordinary speech, and how to weigh one person's reachable embodiment against costs imposed on others.

The reading of Heidegger takes his structure of possibility and departs from his conclusions about the body and about world-poverty; on his own distinction, the restrictions studied here are ontic. The biological evidence comes from a small number of laboratories and organisms, and the human-cell results concern constructs in culture, not transformed persons. The survey evidence is American, bivariate and drawn from reports by one organization. A survey-weighted model of the 2021 respondent-level data with ideology and demographic controls would show how much of the party difference survives adjustment; those data require a Pew account and were not used here.

The most direct next step is the comparative design stated with the propositions: one embodiment technology, fixed route, several institutional regimes, and measurement of all four layers, including the transition period and the possibility of return. That design would show whether income or the non-price components of access bind in practice, and it would turn the concept of ontological impoverishment from a definition into a measured quantity.

## Code and data availability

The analysis code, frozen transcriptions of the published tables and the recorded execution are in https://github.com/piatra-institute/papers-ontological-impoverishment (folder `simulation/`). The inputs are aggregate percentages and margins transcribed from two reports published by Pew Research Center: "U.S. Public Wary of Biomedical Technologies to 'Enhance' Human Abilities" (July 2016; survey of March 2016) and "AI and Human Enhancement: Americans' Openness Is Tempered by a Range of Concerns" (March 2022; survey of November 2021), each row with its printed page. The report PDFs are downloaded by a separate script, identified by SHA-256 hash and not redistributed; Pew's respondent-level datasets were not used.
