# Brief

## Question

What exactly is foreclosed when a society narrows the ways in which a person can come to be embodied, and how does that foreclosure differ from material poverty? A second, empirical question tests a political hypothesis supplied with the seed: is opposition to morphological freedom and to technological abundance a feature of the political left?

## Claim

Ontological impoverishment can be defined as the institutionally avoidable foreclosure of reachable embodiments: bodily organizations that are physically possible, reachable by a known developmental or technical route, livable for the person given feasible support, and yet inaccessible under actual institutions. Formalised as a reachable set under four kinds of constraint (physical possibility, developmental reachability, lived integration, political accessibility), the concept is logically independent of material poverty, makes improvements complementary across layers, and makes intermediate states of a transition count. Each layer has measurable indicators. Heidegger supplies the structure of being-ahead-of-itself and thrownness but not the political claim; his own notion of world-poverty is explicitly non-quantitative and must be set aside. Levin's experiments widen physical possibility and developmental reachability for cells and tissues of particular organisms; they say nothing about lived integration or access for persons. On the political hypothesis, published Pew aggregates for U.S. adults contradict it: Democrats were more often excited than Republicans about every one of six enhancement possibilities, religiosity divided opinion about twice as much as party, and Democrats more often feared under-regulation. The provocation conflates enthusiasm for transformation with preferences about its governance.

## Strongest serious alternative

Capability theory already covers the ground: the body enters as a personal conversion factor (Sen, Robeyns) and Nussbaum's combined capabilities join internal powers to external conditions, so "ontological impoverishment" is capability deprivation renamed. The paper's reply: in those accounts the body is a parameter that converts resources into functionings, while here the embodiment itself is a state variable whose reachable successors are the object of evaluation, with a developmental layer (whether any route exists) and a path condition (whether intermediate states are livable and accessible) that capability sets do not carry. A second rival: Sandberg's negative-rights account of morphological freedom suffices. Reply: a negative right protects against interference but is silent on routes, integration and non-price access; More's original definition was an ability, and the two have drifted apart.
